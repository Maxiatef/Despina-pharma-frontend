PRODUCTION PLANNING

Build a production plan that can grow.

A larger order introduces more decisions across materials, components, inventory and cash flow. Plan the connections before the volume changes.

01

Start with the demand picture

Separate confirmed orders from estimates. Show the expected demand by product, pack size and sales channel, with a date and a confidence level. Add planned promotions or retailer launches that could change the pattern. A forecast becomes useful when the team can see both the opportunity and the uncertainty.

02

Look beyond the filling line

A production slot is only one dependency. A distinctive closure, a specialist ingredient, a printed carton or an outside test can determine when the whole order is ready. Build a list of those dependencies with an owner, an expected arrival and the latest date at which a decision can be made.

03

Use inventory deliberately

Review what is already available, what is committed to other orders and what remains usable for the next run. Consider the shelf life and storage needs of both materials and finished goods. Extra stock should have a clear purpose, such as covering a variable replenishment period, rather than being an automatic response to uncertainty.

04

Keep the specification stable

Growth makes small inconsistencies expensive. Work from one approved record for the formula, components, net contents, artwork, case pack and release criteria. If something changes, record who approved it and whether new samples or evaluation are required. Teams should be able to identify the current revision without reconstructing an email thread.

05

Plan money alongside materials

Map purchase commitments, deposits, production payments and the expected timing of customer receipts. Discuss who owns unused components and how quantity changes affect the order. A bigger run may improve one cost while increasing cash tied up in stock; review the full operating picture before committing.

06

Make progress visible

Agree on a short, regular update covering open decisions, material readiness, schedule changes and the next milestone. Define which issues need immediate escalation and who can resolve them. Useful visibility is specific: a named component awaiting approval tells you more than a general percentage-complete estimate.

Bring these questions to the conversation.

  • Can the next run use the approved specification?
  • Which component or material has the longest dependency?
  • What demand is confirmed, and what is forecast?
  • Who owns purchasing, release and shipment decisions?
  • What would trigger a change to quantity or timing?
